From the first spark of the idea, through planning and execution, to the final touches, this project came together within a single month, yet the thoughts behind it have probably been with me for all twenty-eight years of my life. For those years I have wrestled with one question: “Who am I?”

We live in a world where a person’s identity often seems to be defined by a single word, “success”. Success is on display everywhere, from LinkedIn and Threads to CVs and every digital platform, crowded with titles, radiant milestones and the most polished versions of ourselves. An ordinary person like me struggled right in the middle of that. For years I rummaged through the cluttered “warehouse” that holds a whole lifetime, just to pull out the shiniest things I could find. I hung them up, stood back, and said with pride, “This is me”, only to realise later that it was a flashy outer coat I had to strain myself to fit into.

There has always been a vast distance between the person I show the world and the person I am when I am left alone with myself. Behind the degree or the CV lie the stories I never dared to tell: long nights doubting my own worth, moments of losing my way in the harsh reality of work, and failures I was never brave enough to call by their names.

If someone asks, “Why choose LinkedIn instead of another platform?”, the story begins with my own experience of working in a B2B environment. Back then, using LinkedIn was mandatory. Yet almost every time I opened the app, I sank into a wave of emotions I could not even name. Have you ever noticed how nearly every post follows the same script? They almost always begin with “I’m thrilled to announce…”, followed by a string of impressive achievements. Living in a society that constantly broadcasts resounding victories, I felt it was time for the quietness of failure to be allowed to speak. I believe success is not the only thing worth naming; sometimes the quiet resilience behind our stumbles deserves to be celebrated too.

This project also became a way to redefine myself, not by halos but by my own scratches, because I believe it is the falls in our past that shape the person we are today. Walking alongside me are the teachers, family and former manager who were present at the exact milestones of my failures. They were there, and still they were willing to let me “fall”, simply because they trusted that the version of me I was becoming would one day be able to catch herself. They offered me perspectives and philosophies of life that, back when I was busy chasing perfection, I had never slowed down enough to hear.

Those People Who Let Me Fall has never been a complaint or an accusation; it has always been my deepest expression of gratitude. Thank you for not wrapping me in a safe cocoon, and for not soothing my ego with hollow reassurances. You chose patience and quiet observation, allowing me to stumble, to touch my own limits, and to gather each piece of the lesson with my own hands as I stood back up. Without those falls, there would not be the version of me standing here today.

And finally, this project is not only for me. I wanted to create A Room to Breathe, a place where you, if you have also struggled to define who you are, might find a companion. My friend, go ahead and fall if you must, because the person you are becoming will surely catch you.

The Angel’s Wings That Carried Me on the Road to My Dreams

Some dreams do not reach their destination on their own. They are carried on wings that look nothing like wings: an old motorbike, the early hours before dawn, a single mother who woke before her two small children opened their eyes, and the same hurried call every morning: “Quick, you two, or you’ll be late for school.” Throughout my school years, my mother drove me to school on an old SYM Angel. I do not remember when it first appeared; I only remember that, together with her, it carried me through almost every sky of my childhood. In Grade 5, at around four or five every afternoon, I would take the number 30 bus from home to an evening exam-preparation class in District 1, getting ready for the entrance exam to a selective school. As soon as she finished work, my mother rode straight there and sat waiting for me under a tree until the class was over. On the way home we talked non-stop. I would chatter about everything I had learned that day, which lessons were interesting, what had made me laugh. My mother was born in the 1970s, a time when Vietnam was changing through great hardship. Children of her generation mostly learned to read, write and do basic sums, and she was no exception. Yet she never stopped listening closely, nodding, now and then asking me a question about stories that were sometimes beyond her, even if she surely did not always understand them all. She may not have understood them with her mind, but she never stopped trying to understand her daughter’s small world with her whole heart. Then the results came. I had not passed. I had always believed that if I worked hard enough I would surely get what I wanted; for the first time, I received a completely different answer. For lower secondary school I went to a school far from home, because my mother had heard from an acquaintance that it was a good one. At the Grade 10 entrance exam, on the day we rode to the school to check the results for the specialised high school, the rain came down in sheets. Standing in front of the results board, once again I could not find my name. I no longer remember what my mother said. I only remember that she did not blame me with a single word, and did not press me to explain why I had failed. In the end I went to another school, also a very good one, but even farther away: eight kilometres each way. The old Angel carried me there and back for all three years of high school. So what remains in my memory of those years is a steady pair of shoulders, a motorbike humming along, heavy rain and every road in the city. Each morning I simply sat behind her, my schoolbag hugged to my chest, sometimes still half asleep, while she quietly steered through the crowded streets to get me to school on time. By then the motorbike was very old. When I started Grade 10, my older brother was beginning his final years of high school. Our extra classes swallowed almost all of the savings my mother had put aside. I still remember days when she did not have even 10,000 đồng in her pocket for petrol. So it was common for the motorbike to cut out halfway along the road because the tank was empty. My mother would calmly lift the seat and blow into the fuel tank so the last few drops would run through. I remember it very clearly, but at the time I did not feel ashamed, and I did not think I was missing out. I was too young to know whether 10,000 đồng was a lot or a little, and even less able to understand how carefully a mother had to stretch every coin to keep those rides rolling to school every day, so that both her children could keep learning. Only when I grew up, started working and earned my first money, did I understand that angels do not always appear in the light. Sometimes they come with an empty wallet and a breath blown into a fuel tank, only to try to get their child to where they need to go. After high school I did not get into medical school as I had hoped, but I was admitted to Biology Education at Ho Chi Minh City University of Education. After about a year I realised it was not right for me, and I decided on my own to leave. I did not tell my mother. When she found out, she was silent for a very long time. In those silences I realised that she, too, had sorrows and things that were hard to say. She later told me that she had not understood why I would decide something so important without sharing it with her. At twenty-two I went to Taiwan to study at university, starting again from the very beginning. By my graduation day I had saved enough money to bring my mother and my brother over for the ceremony. I do not know what she was thinking then, but the tears in her eyes as she looked at me that day said it for her: how proud she was of me. That moment will always be one of the most precious of my life. Later, when I had the chance to ask her about the past, my mother told me about a hill. She said everyone has a hill of their own. I had once climbed a hill with her, where the paths were steep and full of stones, and I fell again and again. She said her job was not always to lift me back up, but to stand close enough that I always knew she was there, beside me at every stage of my life. The only thing she could do was show me another path, flatter but farther and a little longer. She believed that even if we arrived late, even if we missed the first brilliant rays of sunrise, in the end the two of us would still be together to watch the last soft light of the day. I thought about that story for a long time. I used to chase the most dazzling sunrise, wanting everything to happen at the right moment and exactly as I had planned. Every time I had to change direction, I felt I was moving more slowly than everyone else. A sense of failure followed me through almost all of my youth. But later, listening to my mother, I suddenly realised that the light of sunset is beautiful too, in a way all its own. Looking back, many things in my life did not turn out as I had hoped. I did not get into the schools I dreamed of, I did not become a doctor, and I did not continue with my first degree. There were countless dreams I had to leave behind, and countless times I had to start again. But through all those turns, I never had to walk alone. My mother could not decide for me what I should study, who I should become or which road I would take. She did not always understand the choices I made. But when I was too young to reach the destination by myself, she gave her time and her strength to take me where I wanted to go. And when I chose a road different from the one she had hoped for, she still let me find my own way. These are the things I most want to remember about my mother. An angel cannot guarantee that I will reach the place I hope for, and cannot walk the whole of my life for me. An angel can only walk beside me for one more stretch of the road, and then trust that I will find the next path myself. My mother has been doing that for a very long time, beginning with the old Angel, with those mornings when I was still half asleep, and with that familiar call: “Quick, little one, or you’ll be late for school.” Thank you, Mom, for every ride you gave me. If one day I travel very far, climb hills I never imagined setting foot on as a child, and look out over the wide world, I know for certain that the journey began long ago, behind my mother’s shoulders, on my mother’s motorbike.

STORY #01 • FROM THAI NGUYEN, VIETNAM

I once felt as if my whole world had collapsed, when I was only fourteen. Today I can smile and talk about it as a lesson I will never forget, but I have never forgotten the shock of that Grade 9 girl. The world in front of her blurred, yet she still had to keep smiling calmly and chatting with her friends. When that failure became known to the whole school, to her friends, her teachers and her relatives (every relationship she had), that girl… only wanted to leave everything behind and disappear. “It was just a literature competition for gifted students, and you didn’t win a prize.” You might shrug and say that, and think it was child’s stuff. But it was the only goal in her young life, the thing everyone was waiting to see her achieve. So when people cry and share their failures online, especially young boys and girls, I always find myself remembering who I was in lower secondary school before I leave any comment. To an adult it may be a tiny failure, but sometimes it is a child’s only dream. Please don’t tell them “Be strong, this doesn’t matter in the grand scheme of life…”, and please don’t scold them. I hope you can say instead: “You tried so hard. When you are tired of crying, eat something filling and go to sleep. Because the Earth keeps turning, time keeps passing, and when tomorrow comes, you can always stand up again whenever you are ready.”

ABSTRACT • REVISED GRADUATION THESIS

Executive Abstract

This study examines how the perceived attractiveness, trustworthiness, and expertise of Key Opinion Leaders (KOLs) on social media relate to young consumers' attitudes and purchase intention in Vietnam. Survey data from 120 Vietnamese respondents (46.7% aged 15–18, 45.0% aged 19–25, and 8.3% aged 26–30) were analyzed using correlation and linear regression analyses in R (version 4.2.1). Trustworthiness was the only influencer characteristic significantly associated with attitude (β = .390, p < .001), and attitude was in turn positively associated with purchase intention (β = .399, p < .001); attractiveness (β = .004, p = .973) and expertise (β = .086, p = .450) showed no unique association with attitude when controlling for trustworthiness. Furthermore, exploratory supplementary regression analyses reconstructed from the correlation matrix indicated that perceived trustworthiness showed a strong direct association with purchase intention (β = .663, p < .001), whereas the indirect path through attitude was modest (standardized indirect effect ≈ .053 to .057, p > .05) and attitude's unique association with purchase intention became marginal once trustworthiness was controlled (β = .133, p = .074). In this sample, perceived trustworthiness was the most consistent correlate of attitude and purchase intention; however, the small convenience sample, the possible overlap between constructs, and the cross-sectional design make these findings exploratory and preclude causal or population-level claims.

Keywords: Key Opinion Leader (KOL), Source Credibility, Consumer Attitude, Purchase Intention (PI), Young consumers in Vietnam.

SECTION 01 • INTRODUCTION (1.1 MARKET CONTEXT & 1.2 RESEARCH GAP)

1.1 Digital Consumption in Vietnam & 1.2 Research Questions

1.1 Market Context: Social media has become a major channel through which brands reach young consumers in Vietnam. As of January 2024, Vietnam had approximately 99.2 million inhabitants, of whom about 78.4 million (79.1%) were internet users, and 72.7 million social media user identities were recorded, equivalent to 73.3% of the population; the median age was 33.0 years (Kemp, 2024). The middle class has reportedly grown from about 15 million people in 2016 to an estimated 33M (BCG) – 44M (Nielsen), as summarised by Adegeest (2023). Together with a young and highly connected population, this makes young Vietnamese consumers an important segment for influencer-based marketing.

1.2 Research Gap & Objectives: Recent studies of Vietnamese consumers report positive effects of influencer credibility attributes on purchase-related outcomes, using PLS-SEM with samples of 269 and 732 respondents (An et al., 2024; Nguyen, 2024). Because the three credibility dimensions of KOLs (attractiveness, trustworthiness, and expertise) tend to be intercorrelated (Section 4.3), examining them separately can obscure which dimension matters most for young consumers. This study tests their unique (partial) associations with attitude and purchase intention within a single model (evaluating social media KOLs in general), addressing two primary research questions:

RQ1: To what extent do perceived attractiveness, trustworthiness, and expertise of social media KOLs uniquely relate to young Vietnamese consumers' attitudes?
RQ2: To what extent is consumers' attitude positively associated with their purchase intention?
SECTION 02 • LITERATURE REVIEW & HYPOTHESIS DEVELOPMENT (2.1–2.6)

Theoretical Foundations & Hypotheses (H1–H4)

  • 2.1 KOLs on Social Media & 2.2 Theory of Planned Behavior (TPB): Online opinion leaders foster parasocial interaction and stronger brand trust than traditional offline celebrities (Djafarova & Trofimenko, 2019; Sokolova & Kefi, 2020; De Veirman et al., 2017). Drawing on TPB (Ajzen, 1991), this study adopts the attitude–intention link alongside Ohanian's (1990) Source Credibility Model.
  • 2.3–2.4 Source Credibility Dimensions: (1) Attractiveness encompasses physical appeal, similarity, familiarity, and likability (Ohanian, 1990; Ellegaard, 2012; Duh & Thabethe, 2021; Xiong et al., 2021; He & Jin, 2022). (2) Trustworthiness reflects whether a communicator is perceived as honest, sincere, dependable, and uncorrupted by self-interested sponsorship (Hovland & Weiss, 1951; Walster et al., 1966; Friedman et al., 1976; Wiedmann & von Mettenheim, 2021). (3) Expertise captures domain knowledge, experience, skills, and qualifications (Hovland et al., 1953; Silvera & Austad, 2004; Amos et al., 2008; Wei & Lu, 2013; Samarasinghe, 2018).
  • 2.5 Attitude & Purchase Intention: Attitude reflects consumers' overall favorable/unfavorable evaluation of KOLs (Greenwald et al., 1968; Schiffman & Kanuk, 1997), while Purchase Intention captures willingness to buy recommended products after evaluation (Hawkins et al., 2004; Malik et al., 2013).
H1: Attractiveness of KOLs is positively associated with consumer attitude.
H2: Trustworthiness of KOLs is positively associated with consumers' attitudes.
H3: Expertise of KOLs is positively associated with consumers' attitudes.
H4: Customers' attitude toward KOLs is positively associated with purchase intention.

3. Methodology & 4.1–4.3 Sample Demographics, Reliability, and Correlations

3.1 DATA COLLECTION & SAMPLING • 3.2 MEASUREMENT

Research Design, G*Power Sensitivity & Measurement

  • Survey Administration & Screening (April 1–10, 2024): Cross-sectional convenience sampling conducted online via Google Forms and offline in Vietnam. Out of 150 initial participants, 120 provided qualified responses meeting the screening condition of prior familiarity with social media KOLs (30 were excluded).
  • Statistical Power Calculation (G*Power): For multiple linear regression with 3 predictors (α = .05, power 1 − β = .80, medium effect size f² = .15), the minimum required sample size is N ≈ 77 (Cohen, 1988). With N = 120, the study could detect effects of f² ≥ .09; smaller unique partial effects may therefore go undetected.
  • Back-Translation & 5-Point Likert Scales: English–Vietnamese back-translation and pilot testing with the supervisor and students were performed prior to distribution. Construct items (1 = Strongly Disagree to 5 = Strongly Agree) adapted Attractiveness (4 items; Duh & Thabethe, 2021; He & Jin, 2022), Trustworthiness (4 items; De Veirman et al., 2017), Expertise (4 items; Chetioui et al., 2020), Attitude (3 items), and Purchase Intention (3 items), with items adapted in part from Kim and Johnson (2016).
  • Methodological Notes: (1) Items assessed perceptions of social media KOLs in general rather than a specific endorser nationality or product category; (2) demographic/behavioral controls were not included in the regressions, and raw observation-level files / verbatim bilingual item wordings are no longer available.

SECTION 05 & SECTION 06 • DISCUSSION, BOUNDARY CONDITIONS & CONCLUSION

5. Discussion of Key Findings & 6. Theoretical Contributions, Managerial Implications & Limitations

5.1 DISCUSSION OF KEY FINDINGS

Why Perceived Trustworthiness Stands Out from Attractiveness & Expertise

After controlling for trustworthiness in multiple regression, neither attractiveness (β = .004 on Attitude; β = .065 on PI) nor expertise (β = .086 on Attitude; β = −.046 on PI) showed a statistically significant unique association, despite positive bivariate correlations (r = .246 to .385, p < .01). While the positive bivariate associations align with He and Jin (2022), the unique dominance of trustworthiness aligns closely with Duh and Thabethe (2021) (who found only trustworthiness uniquely predicted brand engagement) and partially with Lou and Yuan (2019).

A possible, untested interpretation: Counterfeit and misrepresented products have been studied in the Vietnamese market (Nguyen & Huynh, 2015). Where such products are salient, young consumers may weigh a KOL’s perceived honesty more heavily. Because perceived counterfeit risk and word-of-mouth referrals were not measured in this survey, this interpretation is speculative and should be tested directly in future research.

Qualification of H4 (TPB Attitude–Intention Link): While Attitude positively predicted Purchase Intention in simple regression (β = .399, R² = .159), aligning with Ajzen (1991) and Cialdini (2001), controlling for Trustworthiness in Models 4–5 reduced Attitude's unique slope to marginal significance (β = .133, p = .074), suggesting that, in this sample, perceiving a KOL as trustworthy is directly associated with willingness to buy above and beyond general attitude.

5.2 ALTERNATIVE EXPLANATIONS & BOUNDARY CONDITIONS

Five Methodological & Statistical Considerations

  • 1. Shared Variance Among Predictors: Attractiveness and Expertise correlated strongly (r = .672), and Attractiveness correlated moderately with Trustworthiness (r = .529). Even with VIFs ≤ 2.13, substantial shared variance reduces the unique variance left for Attractiveness and Expertise once Trustworthiness is controlled.
  • 2. Common Method Variance (CMV): Because all five constructs were measured concurrently through self-report ratings from the same respondents, the strong correlation between Trustworthiness and Purchase Intention (r = .679) may partially reflect common method variance.
  • 3. Statistical Power & Scale Attenuation: With N = 120, the models had adequate power for medium-to-large effects (β = .390) but limited power for small unique effects (such as β = .086 for Expertise). Moreover, because Attractiveness (α = .72) and Attitude (α = .70) were right at the .70 threshold, measurement error may have attenuated their estimated regression slopes.
  • 4. Sample Composition: The convenience sample skewed toward male respondents (63.3%) and high school / college students (46.7% aged 15–18; 45.0% aged 19–25), whose purchasing power and evaluation criteria may differ from older or higher-income segments.
  • 5. Possible Construct Overlap (Discriminant Validity): Dividing each correlation by the square root of the product of the two scales’ Cronbach’s alphas gives approximate disattenuated correlations of about .84 for Trustworthiness–Purchase Intention and .84 for Attractiveness–Expertise, close to the level (about .85) at which discriminant validity is commonly questioned. Without item-level data, a CFA or HTMT assessment could not be run, so the dominant role of Trustworthiness may partly reflect conceptual overlap between trusting a KOL’s recommendations and being willing to buy.
6.1 THEORETICAL CONTRIBUTIONS & 6.2 MANAGERIAL IMPLICATIONS

Academic Takeaways & Brand Selection Strategy

6.1 Summary & Theoretical Scope: Comparing Ohanian's (1990) three credibility dimensions simultaneously among young Vietnamese social media users demonstrates that perceived trustworthiness is the primary dimension uniquely associated with both attitude (β = .390) and purchase intention (β = .663). Crucially, statistically non-significant partial coefficients for attractiveness and expertise indicate an absence of evidence for a unique partial effect rather than definitive proof of zero effect. Moreover, this study tested only the attitude–intention link rather than the full TPB model (omitting subjective norms and perceived behavioral control).

6.2 Managerial Implications: The findings tentatively suggest that perceived trustworthiness deserves particular weight when selecting KOL partners for campaigns aimed at young Vietnamese consumers. Given the small convenience sample, the possible overlap between constructs and the correlational design, these implications are suggestive rather than prescriptive. Because attractiveness and expertise still showed positive bivariate correlations with attitude and purchase intention, marketers should retain them as baseline screening criteria for initial audience appeal and product-category fit.

6.3 LIMITATIONS & FUTURE RESEARCH DIRECTIONS

Three Core Methodological Boundaries

  • 1. Research Design & Theoretical Scope: Cross-sectional data capture correlational associations rather than causality. Future studies should incorporate full TPB constructs (subjective norms, perceived behavioral control, actual purchase behavior), use raw-data bootstrapping / SEM, and include variables such as eWOM, parasocial interaction, and perceived counterfeit risk (given Model 1 R² = .189).
  • 2. Measurement & Data Availability: Because the raw dataset and original Google Form are no longer available, M, SD, item factor loadings, and CFA validity indices (CR, AVE) could not be computed, education counts in Table 1 were reconciled against reported percentages, and findings apply to KOLs in general rather than Korean brand ambassadors or luxury goods specifically.
  • 3. Sampling, Generalizability & Research Ethics: Convenience sampling (N = 120; 63.3% male, 45.0% high school, 8.3% aged 26–30) limits generalizability. Future research should recruit larger, balanced cohorts and formally document institutional ethical review and guardian consent procedures when surveying minors under 18.

01 / PHILANTHROPIC CONTEXT, SECONDARY RESEARCH & PROBLEM STATEMENT

Generosity Amid Charity Skepticism

Launched in 2014 by social activist Hoàng Hoa Trung and the Niềm Tin (“Faith”) Volunteer Group, Project “Nuôi Em” identified the root cause driving ethnic minority children across 12 northern highland provinces to drop out of school: severe midday hunger forcing children to leave class to forage or work. By funding nutritious hot lunches prepared directly by village teachers at 8,500 VND/meal (150,000 VND/month or 1,450,000 VND/academic year, including a 100,000 VND infrastructure contribution), the initiative created a powerful incentive keeping children in school, expanding into classroom and clean-water construction under “Nuôi Em – Ánh Sáng Núi Rừng”.

• Secondary Literature Baseline (Vũ et al., 2015; Dang et al., 2011; Bekkers & Wiepking, 2011 — Appendices I–V): Shaped by communal solidarity and moral causality (nhân quả; Gillespie & Nicholson, 2005), 75%–88% of Vietnamese adults (ages 18–78) value charity, with religious believers donating nearly 2.7x more annually (~405,000 VND vs. ~150,000 VND). However, to minimize fraud risk, donors route funds through government agencies (29.5%), neighborhood networks (26.4%), or official “Fund for the Poor” programs (24.0%)—leaving independent NGOs with only 10.7% participation. Giving also skews toward immediate poverty relief (80%) and disaster aid (67%) over long-term education (24%) and child sponsorship (15%).

• 2020–2021 Celebrity Charity Scandals (Appendix VI): Following the October 2020 Central Vietnam floods, artist Hoài Linh publicly acknowledged in late May 2021 a 6-month delay in disbursing 14 billion VND (~19 million TWD / ~$600,000 USD), completing transfers only in June 2021 after intense public backlash. Our Semrush Keyword Magic Tool audit for “từ thiện” (49,310 monthly searches across 5,345 keyword variations) showed scandal-related queries near the top of the list (“hoài linh từ thiện”, “thủy tiên từ thiện”, “công an điều tra từ thiện”). The five visible scandal-related keywords make up about 11% of the total search volume, so the audit shows scandal associations at the top of the list rather than a market-wide takeover of search.

• Dual Academic Problem Statement: (1) Case Study Analysis — How did Project “Nuôi Em” apply Grunig & Hunt’s Two-Way Symmetrical model in its 2022 restructuring to institutionalize transparency? (2) Mock IMC Proposal — What supplementary governance refinements and zero-budget IMC tactics can our student team propose to expand young urban donor participation?

02 / CASE AUDIT: TWO-WAY SYMMETRICAL PR (GRUNIG & HUNT, 1984)

The 2022 Restructuring as the Project Described It vs. Proposed Upgrades

In its early years (2014–2020), Project “Nuôi Em” collected donations into founder Hoàng Hoa Trung’s personal bank account. In 2022, drawing on advice from experienced fundraisers and on comments in online charity groups, the founder and the Niềm Tin Volunteer Group restructured the project around four mechanisms. The original report read them as two-way symmetrical communication (Grunig & Hunt, 1984); this edition labels each one as reported, observed or proposed, and pairs it with our recommendations:

  • 1. Dedicated MB Bank Account ('2014') [Real-World Case Study] & Third-Party Auditing [Proposed Upgrade]: Eliminated personal-account fundraising by opening a dedicated project account at MB Bank (Military Commercial Joint Stock Bank) ending in customizable suffix ‘2014’ (founding year). Our proposal recommends formalizing state-affiliated third-party financial auditing and publishing dedicated financial disclosure landing pages.
  • 2. The 1-to-1 “NE Code” (Mã Nuôi Em) Sponsorship Protocol [Real-World Case Study]: Every highland student is assigned a unique alphanumeric NE Code matched 1-to-1 with a single primary sponsor (‘anh nuôi / chị nuôi’) via a 2-minute Fanpage Chatbot. Sponsors include their NE Code in transfer memos; as the project described it, this let organisers reconcile transfers and keep one sponsor per child. In June 2026 the founder acknowledged that codes had been assigned manually in Excel, producing wrong and duplicate codes (see the Postscript).
  • 3. Direct Contact & Village Sub-Groups [Real-World Case Study]: Each sponsor receives a full digital profile with photos and direct phone numbers of the child's parents, classroom teachers, and village headman (trưởng bản), can call teachers anytime, join highland field visits (3x/year), and view monthly meal clips in village-specific Facebook sub-groups (Mường Tè, Bát Xát, Bảo Lâm)—driving 49,000+ Fanpage followers, 21,500+ group members, and 1.8M TikTok views.
  • 4. Brand Identity ('Ánh Sáng Núi Rừng') & Web Portal (nuoiem.com) [Real + Student Proposal]: Alongside nuoiem.com (showcasing workflows and merit certificates from the Vietnam Youth Federation and UN Volunteers) and the warm orange flame logo, our proposal recommends switching niemtingroup@gmail.com to nuoiem@gmail.com, staffing a 24/7 hotline, and adding an English–Vietnamese bilingual interface.

POSTSCRIPT • NOVEMBER 2026

Developments after this report

The analysis above reflects information available in January 2023. This section records later public developments drawn only from press reports; primary documents were not reviewed, and individual donors’ allegations reported in the press remain unverified.

What was reported

  • 6–7 December 2025: questions about the project’s financial transparency circulated on social media; on 7 December the founder announced a temporary suspension and froze the project account to reconcile income and expenses (Báo Văn hóa, 2025).
  • 9–10 December 2025: the project acknowledged data-compilation errors and limitations in its financial reporting, and denied cutting meals, merging sponsors onto one child, or creating fake codes (Znews, 2025).
  • 15 December 2025: the Ministry of Public Security directed police in 13 provinces and cities to review the project (Viettimes, 2025).
  • 24 April 2026: the Ministry’s Criminal Police Department decided not to open a criminal case, finding no signs of a crime (Tuổi Trẻ, 2026; Báo Thanh Niên, 2026).
  • 6 June 2026: the founder’s statement acknowledged that the project had operated through a personal bank account with pooled cash flows, had not applied accounting standards or had independent audits, and had assigned sponsor codes manually in Excel, producing wrong and duplicate codes. From 1 June 2026 the project became a non-profit social enterprise, committing to publish financial statements and undergo independent audits (Cafebiz, 2026).

What this changes in my reading

  • Open communication is not verifiable accountability. I would now present the 2022 changes as evidence of communication openness, not of institutionalised transparency.
  • A described mechanism is not an audited one. “One child, one sponsor” was a design stated by the project; a sound case study separates how a mechanism is designed from how it performs.
  • Growth should follow back-office capacity: governance first (independent audit, regular reconciliation, published statements), campaigns second.
  • Evaluation should measure accountability, not only reach: duplicate or erroneous codes, reconciliation lag, complaint-resolution time, on-schedule audited statements.

I used to view transparency as a window: open the shutters, show donors the children and their meals, and trust would follow. What happened after this report taught me that a window is no substitute for a key, here an accounting ledger that independent eyes can open. Photographs and updates can make an organisation look open; only independent records can make it accountable. If I wrote it again, I would stop asking, “How openly does this organisation communicate?” and ask instead, “Who else is in a position to verify it?”

When you arrive in Taiwan as an international student, one of the first things you notice is how naturally sacred spaces sit beside everyday life: a centuries-old Taoist temple stands right down the street from a bus stop, and two floors above a busy university hallway, a quiet room is set aside for Muslim students to lay down their prayer mats. Produced for our Introduction to Media and Entertainment Business Management course at I-Shou University, this documentary started from our wish to look beyond the formal record and ask what coexistence actually feels like in the gestures of casting wooden moon blocks (Jiaobei) or unlocking a campus prayer room door.

Taiwan’s religious life is known for its diversity and tolerance. Some observers compare its many faiths to threads that together form one cultural tapestry. Freedom of religious belief is protected by Article 13 of the Constitution of the Republic of China (Taiwan), and in Freedom House’s Freedom in the World 2018 report Taiwan received the maximum score (4/4) for religious freedom, with the note that “Taiwanese of all faiths can worship freely.” (Freedom House, 2018 ↗)

LOCATION 01 • TRADITIONAL FOLK & TAOIST HERITAGE

1. Kaohsiung Guandi Temple (高雄關帝廟)

📍 Address: No. 52, Wumiao Road, Lingya District, Kaohsiung City
🕒 Open Time: 6:00 AM – 10:00 PM  |  📞 Phone: 07 721 8782
🚌 Transportation: From I-Shou University, take Bus No. 8505 from E-Da World Station to Martial Arts Stadium Station, then walk 450m (~6 minutes) to the temple.

Featured Cultural Rituals & Activities:

• Praying for Love at the Yue Lao (Temple of the Moon) Hall: Located on the 2nd floor at the end of the left-side hall. In Taiwanese folk belief, Yue Lao (the matchmaker deity) binds destined unmarried men and women together with an invisible red string recorded in the marriage book. Worshippers seeking marriage offer a 200 NTD spiritual service to pray for a blessed connection.

• Casting Moon Blocks / Divination Boards (擲筊 Jiaobei): Worshippers frame a question in their mind and cast a pair of crescent wooden blocks three times to receive the deity's answer:
  — One flat-side up + one rounded-side up (Shengjiao): Yes (the deity agrees).
  — Both rounded-side up (Yinjiao): No (negative response).
  — Both flat-side up (Xiaojiao): The deity smiles or feels the question is frivolous/already clear.

PROJECT 06 • CREATIVE THINKING • CULTURAL LOCALIZATION

Ô Ăn Quan: Sharing a Vietnamese Folk Game Across Cultures

Fall 2021 · I-Shou University, Creative Thinking and Innovation course · Team project

How do you introduce a game someone has never played before, without losing what makes it worth playing? Growing up in Vietnam, Ô Ăn Quan needed almost nothing: chalk on a concrete courtyard, a handful of pebbles, two friends sitting cross-legged. A translated rulebook would never have brought my Taiwanese classmates into that courtyard. So our team rebuilt the board around the map of Taiwan and decorated each square with a Taiwanese local specialty, while keeping the capturing rhythm that makes the game Vietnamese.

01 / The adaptation question

How do we turn a traditional Vietnamese courtyard game into something players in Taiwan can step into, without losing the strategic rhythm that makes it Vietnamese?

02 / Rebuilding the board

The physical board uses the map of Taiwan as its base, with a local specialty food drawn in each square, plus an English rulebook and a 60-second visual onboarding.

03 / What stayed with me

Watching classmates play the prototype left me with a question: when a cultural product crosses a border, where is the line between thoughtful adaptation and losing what made it distinctive?

2022–2024 • ACADEMIC RECOGNITION

Dean’s List Award

International College, I-Shou University

I didn’t know the Dean’s List existed when I arrived. The first year I qualified, I missed it, and my cousin received it. In our family chat, I felt embarrassed enough to make it my own goal. I earned it two years in a row.

Read the story

I did not even know the Dean’s List existed when I first arrived at I-Shou University. As a spring-entry student, I initially found out about the recognition only to discover that I was not eligible, since the results were calculated based on a full academic year. The first year I was eligible, however, I missed it. I had made choices that did not always work in my academic favour: moving farther from campus, taking courses outside my major, and occasionally choosing classes for reasons that had more to do with my personal life than my academic priorities.

That same year, my older cousin received the recognition. As relatives congratulated her in our family group chat, they also asked about me. I remember feeling embarrassed, but also quietly deciding that I would earn that recognition myself. I eventually made it onto the Dean’s List for two consecutive years. When I received the first notification, I scrolled all the way back through the family chat and replied directly to the message my uncle had sent a year earlier, asking about my academic results.

Looking back, I know my relationship with academic achievement was shaped partly by growing up in a family where doing well in school carried considerable weight, and recognition often followed good grades. My motivation was not always pure intellectual curiosity. Sometimes it was the fear of falling behind, the desire to prove myself, or even a little envy. I no longer feel the need to disguise those motivations as something more admirable. They were part of what kept me going, alongside a growing interest in what I was learning.

By graduation, I ranked second in a class of 18, with a GPA of 3.99/4.00 (95.38/100). The two consecutive Dean’s List recognitions remain meaningful to me, not because they tell the whole story of my academic journey, but because I remember exactly why earning them once mattered so much to me.

MARCH 2023 • INTERNATIONAL EXCHANGE

14th Socially Innovative Global Classroom (90 hours)

Osaka Metropolitan University, Japan · Online programme

A friend’s invitation introduced me to the SDGs, which I barely knew. For my project I chose southern Vietnam, where I grew up, and found river sand being dug up for construction, with saltwater intrusion and the threat of rising seas following.

Read the story

I joined the programme because a friend studying International Business Administration invited me. At the start I did not even know what the Sustainable Development Goals were. The programme asked us to carry out a project on these goals, tied to the region where we live. Instead of choosing Taiwan, I decided to focus on southern Vietnam, because I was born and raised in Ho Chi Minh City.

Researching it, I uncovered an alarming problem in the Mekong Delta: widespread, uncontrolled sand extraction for construction. It has led to saltwater intrusion across the Delta, and it raises the very real risk that the region could be swallowed by the sea as sea levels rise. Somewhere in that project I realised I knew almost nothing about the place I came from. It also showed me how much awareness needs to reach young people, and then the wider community. Sitting in virtual breakout rooms with peers across Asia, I saw how the same issue looks very different depending on the street you grew up on.

In the Cultural Ambassador: X-Gen Competition I was responsible for promoting the contest before, during and after the event. It is an annual competition. In the two years before, the programme had a dedicated marketing team; during my term as Vice President of Marketing of the International Student Union, the work fell under my management. That year the structure and content of the competition had also been updated, becoming more complex and calling for a much deeper, more detailed promotion strategy.

I coordinated outreach across several channels and tied the competition to the other cultural events the campus had run before, by month, by quarter and by semester: Cultural Festival, Global Lounge, Taiwan Exploration, the Public Speaking Competition. After that year the competition was not held again, because the scale was simply too large: 30 international contestants, from 18 October to 31 May.

Before the new season began, I personally edited a recap video of the previous season to promote the new edition, and I designed graphics that were printed as banners across the school. While the competition ran, my team and I kept information updated, attended the rounds and the related cultural activities to capture images, and published content online. It was a very complex process that needed a large team of members from many different countries.
